Executive Summary

Bottom line: the demand is real, documented, and under-served in exactly MRA's lane.

Canada presents a credible, well-documented opportunity for a mobile medical trailer provider offering leasing, transport/logistics, and maintenance. Demand is driven by verified surgical and diagnostic-imaging backlogs, structural rural/remote/northern access gaps, and a rural ER staffing crisis. Multiple provinces already run real mobile-health programs at meaningful scale — and, critically for MRA's service model, Canadian hospitals already pay for exactly the services MRA sells: in BC's provincial mobile-imaging program, the requesting hospital bears the transport, setup and accreditation costs, and demand outstrips a two-trailer fleet with six-month lead times.

The strongest single signal: BC's health authority runs two mobile MRI/CT trailers on a first-come-first-served reservation system — sites must book 6 months in advance and pay all deployment costs (transport, setup/construction, accreditation, training, supplies). A booked-out government fleet with hospitals footing the logistics bill is unmet demand wearing a lab coat.

The competitive field for trailer-format, lease-plus-operations providers serving Canada appears thin: the visible players sell small vans (no leasing or operations) or advertise pan-Canada imaging rental with no verified Canadian footprint. The main evidence gap: no reliable Canada-specific market-size figure exists — demand must be argued from waitlists, deployments and procurement behaviour (which this briefing does).

Demand Drivers

Four structural forces pushing care onto wheels

1 · Surgical & imaging backlogs (national, verified)

68% / 61%of hip / knee replacements done within the 6-month benchmark in 2024 — still below pre-pandemic levelsCIHI, June 2025
+15 daysgrowth in median MRI wait 2019→2024, even though scan volumes rose 16%CIHI, June 2025
Demand > capacitysurgical volumes rose ~21–26% vs 2019 and waits still grew — fixed infrastructure isn't keeping upCIHI, June 2025

This is precisely the gap mobile MRI/CT and surgical-support units exist to close: capacity that arrives in months, not the years a bricks-and-mortar build takes.

2 · Rural & northern access gaps (structural, not cyclical)

~$27,400per-capita health spend in Nunavut vs ~$9,000 nationally — yet the territories report Canada's poorest outcomesCIHI 2024 · peer-reviewed studies
No road, no hospitalmany Inuit communities lack year-round road access and few have hospitals — advanced care means flying southHealthcare (2020), corroborated 2024
2 wks / 6one studied remote NWT community sees a nurse 2 weeks per 6-week cycle; a physician 3 daysInt J Circumpolar Health

The paradox works in mobile's favour: the money being spent on medevac and patient travel is money that could fund bringing the clinic to the community instead. Federal precedent exists at scale — Budget 2018 committed $1.5 billion over five years (plus $149M/yr ongoing) to remote Indigenous health access. That window has closed, but it demonstrates the funding appetite for this exact problem.

3 · Rural ER closures & the staffing crisis

CBC's December 2024 analysis found roughly 1 in 5 Ontario hospitals with ERs or urgent-care centres (38 of 176) experienced temporary closures between January 2022 and November 2024 — overwhelmingly rural, with nurse shortages driving more than 85% of closure hours. The ten hardest-hit ERs are all in communities under 8,000 people. The trend is national ("from Nova Scotia to Quebec to British Columbia" — Canadian Association of Emergency Physicians), and provinces are spending to plug it: Ontario pledged $44M on top of $90M/yr, plus $500M+ for nurse training.

Mobile units don't fix staffing — but they let a health authority concentrate scarce staff where patients are, support community paramedicine, and keep screening/diagnostics running when a small facility can't.

4 · Primary-care shortage

Millions of Canadians lack a regular primary-care provider (widely reported at ~6.5M in national surveys) — the driver behind provincial interest in mobile outreach clinics, and the copy already used on the concept sites.

Market Size

Global numbers are healthy; a Canada-specific figure doesn't exist yet

Five commercial research firms size the global mobile clinics market at roughly USD 3.3–6.2 billion (2024–2026), growing at ~7–10% CAGR, with North America the largest region (~36% of revenue). One outlier report claims 23% CAGR and should be ignored.

Honest caveat: no Canada-specific market-size or growth figure survived our verification pass — the global reports don't split Canada out. Any pitch deck should argue Canadian demand from the waitlist data, deployments and procurement behaviour in this briefing rather than quoting a dollar TAM. A paid market-research report could close this gap if a number is ever needed.
Proven Canadian Deployments

Mobile health in Canada is not a pilot — it's load-bearing infrastructure

British Columbia · PHSA Provincial Medical Imaging Office

Mobile MRI + CT trailers, booked solid

Two mobile imaging trailers (a GE Signa Voyager 1.5T MRI and a GE Revolution Maxima CT, purchased 2023) rotate among hospital sites on a reservation model: sites book 4–6 months (including ~2 months of setup, construction, acceptance testing and accreditation), must request 6 months in advance, first-come-first-served. The MRI has been in service since Nov 2024 — 230+ patients early on, up to ~3,265 exams/yr capacity.

Why it matters to MRA: requesting hospitals pay all deployment costs — transport and delivery fees, construction/setup, accreditation, training, supplies. Canadian hospitals are already paying for MRA's exact service menu, inside a program whose fleet is too small for its demand.

Saskatchewan · Saskatchewan Health Authority

A health authority already leases a mobile MRI from a third party

The Regina General Hospital mobile MRI is leased by the SHA from National Medical Imaging — a James Smith Cree Nation–owned company — backed by a $2.4M provincial allocation. Saskatchewan's two mobile mammography trailers were foundation-funded at ~$2.2M per unit.

Why it matters to MRA: this is the direct precedent for the lease model — a provincial health authority signing a lease with a private (and notably, Indigenous-owned) mobile-unit provider. It also hints that Indigenous-partnership structures may be a procurement advantage worth exploring.

Alberta · Alberta Cancer Foundation + Primary Care Alberta ("Screen Test")

Foundation-funded fleet renewal, September 2025

Two new mobile mammography units — 53-foot trailers — funded by the Alberta Cancer Foundation with the Grande Prairie Regional and Northern Lights Health Foundations, operated by the provincial Screen Test program. They serve 120+ rural and Indigenous communities (28 Indigenous) annually; the program has run since 1991 and delivered 500,000+ mammograms. The new trailers replaced decade-old units.

Why it matters to MRA: the Canadian pattern is philanthropic capital + health-authority operation — and aging fleets mean a recurring replacement and refurbishment market, plus maintenance contracts on 30-year-old programs that can't afford downtime.

British Columbia · BC Cancer (PHSA)

Mobile units carry ~10% of an entire province's screening volume

BC Cancer operates three digital mammography coaches visiting 170+ rural communities including 40+ First Nations communities every year — performing roughly 10% of ALL screening mammograms in British Columbia.

Why it matters to MRA: proof at provincial scale that mobile delivery is a permanent operating model in Canada, not an experiment. (Note: these are self-propelled coaches, not towed trailers.)

British Columbia · Provincial Health Services Authority

A standby "hospital on wheels" kept ready 24/7

The BC Mobile Medical Unit — described by PHSA as Canada's first mobile health facility — is owned by PHSA, operated through Health Emergency Management BC, and kept deployment-ready around the clock for emergency response, business continuity (backstopping facilities during disasters/renovations), clinical education and mobile consultation.

Why it matters to MRA: Canadian health systems demonstrably value and fund standby mobile capacity and run mobile-unit logistics as a discipline — a template MRA can sell into provinces that don't want to own and operate it themselves.

Business Opportunities

Six plays for the lease + logistics + maintenance model

  1. Imaging surge capacity. Lease mobile MRI/CT to provinces whose own trailers are booked six months out — or contract the transport/setup/accreditation legs the hospitals already pay for on government-owned units.
  2. Fleet replacement & refurbishment. Alberta just replaced decade-old trailers; BC's coaches and Saskatchewan's units age on the same clock. Bid the replacements; sell maintenance on whatever wins.
  3. Maintenance contracts on existing fleets. Every provincial program owns units that need exactly the Essential Maintenance package — DOT inspection, generator, HVAC, hydraulics — without owning a service bay.
  4. Standby emergency capacity. BC funds a 24/7-ready mobile hospital; most provinces don't have one. Offer a leased standby unit + readiness maintenance + activation logistics as an annual contract.
  5. Foundation-funded capital projects. Health foundations buy the trailer, the authority operates it, and someone must design, build, deliver and maintain it. Court the foundations directly with turnkey proposals.
  6. Indigenous-partnership leasing. The SHA already leases its mobile MRI from a First Nation–owned company. Explore joint ventures with Indigenous partners — it fits federal funding priorities and observed procurement preferences.
What We Still Need to Learn

Open questions before a full go-to-market

  • Ontario, Québec, Manitoba: the biggest markets weren't covered by verified deployments in this pass. Scan the Ontario Tenders Portal, SEAO (Québec) and Shared Health Manitoba for mobile-unit tenders from the last 3 years.
  • Lease vs. own: will authorities contract third-party lease-plus-operate at scale, or do they prefer owning and buying only services? (The SHA lease says yes-sometimes; more data points needed.)
  • Regulatory path for US-built units: Health Canada device licensing, provincial diagnostic accreditation (e.g. BC's DAP), oversize/transport permits, and staffing/licensure rules for operated units crossing provincial lines.
  • A defensible Canadian TAM figure, if investor/board material ever needs one (paid research or a bottom-up build from provincial fleets).
Verification notes: one widely-repeated claim (a 58%/32% nurse-led vs physician-led statistic for remote Indigenous communities) failed adversarial verification and is excluded. The $1.5B federal commitment is a closed (2018–2023) window — cite it as precedent, not an open program. Competitor claims rest partly on the companies' own marketing pages. The strongest deployments are government-owned — they prove systems fund mobile capacity and pay for logistics, and only the Saskatchewan lease directly proves third-party leasing.
Side Note

On the mrahealthcare.ca domain

Keep it — it's the right call. A .ca is a trust signal for provincial buyers and foundations (and CIRA-verified Canadian presence reads well in tenders), and "mrahealthcare" matches the logo lockup exactly. Two suggestions: (1) also register mrahealthcare.com if available and redirect it to the .ca, so US referrals and typos don't dead-end; (2) once live, have goMRA.com/healthcare link prominently to the Canadian site so the two businesses reinforce rather than compete. Alternatives like mramedical.ca or mobilemedical.ca are weaker: they either lose the brand or are too generic to own.
